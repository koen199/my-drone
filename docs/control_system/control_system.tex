	\section{Altitude Controller}
	The altitude controller employs a proportional (P) control scheme to map a vertical position error to a target vertical velocity. Receiving the estimated altitude from the state estimator, the controller computes the target vertical speed $v_{z,\mathrm{sp}}$ according to:
	\begin{equation}
		v_{z,\mathrm{sp}}(t) = K_p \left( p_{z,\mathrm{sp}}(t) - p_{z,\mathrm{m}}(t) \right)
	\end{equation}
	where $p_{z,\mathrm{sp}}$ is the setpoint altitude, $p_{z,\mathrm{m}}$ is the estimated vertical position, and $K_p$ is the proportional position gain.
	
	The output $v_{z,\mathrm{sp}}(t)$ serves directly as the setpoint for the inner-loop vertical speed controller derived in Section 1.2. To ensure flight safety and prevent actuator saturation during large position steps, the commanded speed is typically constrained by maximum climb and sink rate limits:
	\begin{equation}
		v_{z,\mathrm{sp,sat}}(t) = \mathrm{sat}_{\left[v_{z,\mathrm{min}}, v_{z,\mathrm{max}}\right]} \left( v_{z,\mathrm{sp}}(t) \right)
	\end{equation}
	
	By cascading this outer-loop position controller into the inner-loop velocity controller, the overall system achieves zero steady-state error to step position commands via the velocity loop's integral action.
	

	\section{Attitude Controller}
	
	We represent the attitude of the drone using the three Euler angles: roll, pitch, and yaw ($\phi, \theta, \psi$).
	To control the attitude, we need to map the desired and actual attitudes to a vector $\bm{e}$ representing a desired 3D angular velocity vector that reduces the orientation error.
	
	We start by converting the roll, pitch, and yaw angles to a rotation matrix ($\left(\phi, \theta, \psi\right) \rightarrow \mathbf{R}$). This is achieved via the standard Z-Y-X rotation sequence:
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}_z(\psi) \cdot \mathbf{R}_y(\theta) \cdot \mathbf{R}_x(\phi)
	\end{equation*}
	where the elementary rotation matrices are defined as:
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathbf{R}_x(\phi) = \begin{bmatrix}
			1 & 0 & 0 \\
			0 & \cos\phi & -\sin\phi \\
			0 & \sin\phi & \cos\phi
		\end{bmatrix}, \quad
		\mathbf{R}_y(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix}
			\cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta \\
			0 & 1 & 0 \\
			-\sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta
		\end{bmatrix}, \quad
		\mathbf{R}_z(\psi) = \begin{bmatrix}
			\cos\psi & -\sin\psi & 0 \\
			\sin\psi & \cos\psi & 0 \\
			0 & 0 & 1
		\end{bmatrix}
	\end{equation*}
	The resulting matrix $\mathbf{R}$ transforms coordinates from the body frame $b$ to the world frame $n$.
	
	Applying this mapping to the measured and desired attitudes yields $\mathbf{R}_m$ and $\mathbf{R}_d$, respectively. The relative rotation error matrix $\mathbf{R}_e$ (expressed in the body frame) is given by:
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathbf{R}_e = \mathbf{R}_m^T \mathbf{R}_d
	\end{equation*}
	The matrix $\mathbf{R}_e$ represents the rotation needed to align the measured body frame with the desired body frame, satisfying $\mathbf{R}_m \mathbf{R}_e = \mathbf{R}_d$.
	
	To compute the correction, we convert $\mathbf{R}_e$ into an axis-angle representation $(\mathbf{u}, \theta)$. The magnitude of the rotation angle $\theta$ is given by:
	\begin{equation*}
		\theta = \arccos\left(\frac{\text{tr}(\mathbf{R}_e) - 1}{2}\right) = \arccos\left(\frac{R_{e,11} + R_{e,22} + R_{e,33} - 1}{2}\right)
	\end{equation*}
	The unit axis of rotation $\mathbf{u}$ is calculated as:
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathbf{u} = \frac{1}{2\sin\theta} \begin{bmatrix} R_{e,32} - R_{e,23} \\ R_{e,13} - R_{e,31} \\ R_{e,21} - R_{e,12} \end{bmatrix}
	\end{equation*}
	
	Finally, the controller uses a proportional (P) control scheme to map the orientation error vector to an angular velocity setpoint $\bm{\omega}_{\text{sp}}$:
	\begin{equation*}
		\bm{\omega}_{\text{sp}} = K_p \cdot \theta \cdot \mathbf{u}
	\end{equation*}
	
	The formulation above introduces a numerical singularity as the attitude error approaches zero ($\theta \to 0$), caused by the division by $2\sin\theta$ when computing $\mathbf{u}$. To avoid division-by-zero errors and eliminate computationally expensive trigonometric operations, we adopt a geometric error representation directly on $SO(3)$.
	
	Using Rodrigues' rotation formula, the antisymmetric component of the error matrix $\mathbf{R}_e$ isolates the rotation axis $\mathbf{u}$ scaled by $\sin\theta$:
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathbf{R}_e - \mathbf{R}_e^T = 2\sin\theta \, [\mathbf{u}]_\times
	\end{equation*}
	where $[\mathbf{u}]_\times$ denotes the skew-symmetric matrix representation of $\mathbf{u}$. Applying the unskew operator $(\cdot)^\vee$ to extract the 3D vector components, we define the geometric orientation error vector $\mathbf{e}_R$:
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathbf{e}_R = \frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{R}_e - \mathbf{R}_e^T)^\vee = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} R_{e,32} - R_{e,23} \\ R_{e,13} - R_{e,31} \\ R_{e,21} - R_{e,12} \end{bmatrix} = \sin\theta \cdot \mathbf{u}
	\end{equation*}
	
	For small angular errors ($\theta \to 0$), $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, making $\mathbf{e}_R$ first-order equivalent to $\theta \cdot \mathbf{u}$. Replacing $\theta \mathbf{u}$ with $\mathbf{e}_R$ yields the updated angular velocity setpoint:
	\begin{equation*}
		\bm{\omega}_{\text{sp}} = K_p \cdot \mathbf{e}_R
	\end{equation*}
	
	This formulation requires no divisions or trigonometric evaluations ($\arccos$, $\sin$), guaranteeing numerical stability across all operational states including $\theta = 0$.
	
	\section{Rate controller}
	
	The rate controller does two things:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Converts a desired rotation speed $\bm{\omega}_{\mathrm{sp}}$ into a torque setpoint $\mathbf{T}$
		\item Converts a desired vertical speed $v_z$ into a verical force setpoint $F_z$
	\end{itemize}
	
	
	\subsection{Rotational Speed Controller}
	
	We employ a proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control scheme operating on the body-rate error vector:
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{e}(t) = \bm{\omega}_{\mathrm{sp}}(t) - \bm{\omega}_{\mathrm{m}}(t)
	\end{equation}
	where $\bm{\omega}_{\mathrm{sp}}$ is the setpoint angular velocity and $\bm{\omega}_{\mathrm{m}}$ is the measured angular velocity.
	
	To make the gain values intuitive, we design the controller such that the proportional action corresponds directly to a desired angular acceleration per unit error:
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\bm{\omega}}_{\mathrm{des}} = \mathbf{K}_p \mathbf{e}(t)
	\end{equation}
	The gain matrix $\mathbf{K}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ is structured with the individual axis gains along the diagonal while off-diagonal elements are zero to decouple the rotational dynamics:
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf{K}_p = 
		\begin{bmatrix}
			K_{p,xx} & 0 & 0 \\
			0 & K_{p,yy} & 0 \\
			0 & 0 & K_{p,zz}
		\end{bmatrix}
	\end{equation}
	For instance, if $K_{p,xx} = 1\text{ s}^{-1}$ and the roll error is $1\text{ rad/s}$, the controller commands a roll angular acceleration of $1\text{ rad/s}^2$.
	
	From Euler's rotational equations of motion (ignoring gyroscopic and cross-coupling terms for control gain allocation), the required torque vector $\bm{T}$ relates to angular acceleration via the moment of inertia matrix $\mathbf{I}$:
	\begin{equation}
		\bm{T} = \mathbf{I} \dot{\bm{\omega}}
	\end{equation}
	
	Premultiplying the target acceleration by $\mathbf{I}$ maps the control law to physical torque commands. Extending this to full PID control yields the control law $\bm{u}(t) \equiv \bm{T}(t)$:
	\begin{equation}
		\bm{u}(t) = \mathbf{I} \mathbf{K}_p \mathbf{e}(t) + \mathbf{I} \mathbf{K}_i \int_0^t \mathbf{e}(\tau) \, d\tau + \mathbf{I} \mathbf{K}_d \frac{d\mathbf{e}(t)}{dt}
	\end{equation}
	where $\mathbf{K}_p, \mathbf{K}_i, \mathbf{K}_d \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ are diagonal gain matrices scaling the desired proportional, integral, and derivative angular accelerations.
	
	\subsection{Vertical Speed Controller}
	
	The vertical speed controller operates analogously to the rotational speed controller, though the body rate vector is replaced by a scalar vertical velocity error:
	\begin{equation}
		e(t) = v_{z,\mathrm{sp}}(t) - v_{z,\mathrm{m}}(t)
	\end{equation}
	where $v_{z,\mathrm{sp}}$ is the target vertical speed and $v_{z,\mathrm{m}}$ is the measured vertical speed.
	
	Following the same design philosophy of physical gain intuitive selection, the controller gains are formulated to directly command a desired vertical acceleration:
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{v}_{z,\mathrm{des}} = K_{p,z} e(t)
	\end{equation}
	This structure allows the controller gains to be interpreted in physical units:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Proportional action ($K_{p,z}$):} A velocity error of $1\text{ m/s}$ commands an acceleration of $K_{p,z}\text{ m/s}^2$.
		\item \textbf{Integral action ($K_{i,z}$):} A persistent error of $1\text{ m/s}$ over $1\text{ s}$ commands an additional acceleration of $K_{i,z}\text{ m/s}^2$.
		\item \textbf{Derivative action ($K_{d,z}$):} A velocity error changing at a rate of $1\text{ m/s}^2$ commands an immediate acceleration of $K_{d,z}\text{ m/s}^2$.
	\end{itemize}
	
	Using Newton's second law ($F = m a$), the commanded target acceleration $\dot{v}_{z,\mathrm{des}}$ maps to a physical vertical force by scaling by the vehicle mass $m$. Extending this to the complete PID formulation yields:
	\begin{equation}
		F_z(t) = m K_{p,z} e(t) + m K_{i,z} \int_0^t e(\tau) \, d\tau + m K_{d,z} \frac{d e(t)}{d t}
	\end{equation}
	
	To improve initial responsiveness, the integral term can be pre-loaded while the drone is still grounded prior to takeoff. Initializing the integrator with a fraction of the vehicle's hovering force—typically around $0.8 \, m g$—prevents significant initial altitude dip or response lag by pre-allocating the baseline force required to counteract gravity.
	\section{Mixer}
	The mixer maps the desired control inputs—the torque vector $\mathbf{T} = [T_x, T_y, T_z]^\top$ and the required vertical force component $F_z$—to the individual motor thrust forces $F_{t1}, F_{t2}, F_{t3},$ and $F_{t4}$.
	
	\begin{figure}[htbp]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{mixer_image_adapted.png}
		\caption{A descriptive caption for your image.}
		\label{fig:my_image}
	\end{figure}
	
	Once the thrust vector $\mathbf{f} = [F_{t1}, F_{t2}, F_{t3}, F_{t4}]^\top$ is determined, each thrust force can be converted into an RPM setpoint using the aerodynamic relationship:
	\[
	F_t = k_{\text{t}} \omega^2
	\]
	where $k_{\text{t}}$ represents the thrust coefficient of the propeller and $\omega$ is the rotational speed.
	
	Assuming a standard quadrotor frame geometry, the roll and pitch torques ($T_x$ and $T_y$) are derived from the individual thrust forces and motor moment arms ($l_1$ and $l_2$):
	\begin{align*}
		T_x &= (F_{t4} + F_{t3} - F_{t1} - F_{t2}) l_1 \\
		T_y &= (F_{t2} + F_{t3} - F_{t1} - F_{t4}) l_2 
	\end{align*}
	
	To determine the yaw torque $T_z$, we must account for the reaction torque $T_r$ generated by propeller which acts on the frame in the opposite direction as the spinning direction:
	\[
	T_r = k_{\text{d}} \omega^2
	\]
	Defining the constant ratio of drag-to-thrust coefficients as:
	\[
	c = \frac{T_r}{F_t} = \frac{k_{\text{d}}}{k_{\text{t}}}
	\]
	the total yaw torque $T_z$ can be expressed directly in terms of individual thrust forces:
	\[
	T_z = c (F_{t2} + F_{t4} - F_{t1} - F_{t3})
	\]
	
	The total thrust magnitude $F$ is the scalar sum of all propeller thrusts:
	\[
	F = F_{t1} + F_{t2} + F_{t3} + F_{t4}
	\]
	Since higher-level controllers output a required vertical force $F_z$, total required thrust $F$ is related to $F_z$ via the current attitude:
	\[
	F = \frac{F_z}{\cos(\phi) \cos(\theta)}
	\]
	where $\phi$ is the roll angle and $\theta$ is the pitch angle.
	
	Combining these relations yields the linear system:
	\[
	\begin{bmatrix}
		F \\
		T_x \\
		T_y \\
		T_z
	\end{bmatrix} = 
	\begin{bmatrix}
		1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
		-l_1 & -l_1 & l_1 & l_1 \\
		-l_2 & l_2 & l_2 & -l_2 \\
		c & -c & c & -c
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		F_{t1} \\
		F_{t2} \\
		F_{t3} \\
		F_{t4}
	\end{bmatrix}
	\]
	
	In compact matrix form, this is written as:
	\[
	\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{f}
	\]
	where $\mathbf{u} = [F, T_x, T_y, T_z]^\top$ is the total control effort vector and $\mathbf{A}$ is the mixer allocation matrix. Provided $\mathbf{A}$ is invertible, the required individual motor thrusts are computed by solving the system:
	\[
	\mathbf{f} = \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{u}
	\]
